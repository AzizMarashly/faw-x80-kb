    Windshield air-intake water leak in rain

    Tags: #water-ingress · #windshield · #hvac

    Prices and suppliers on this page are Russian-market (RUB, at the dates shown). Verify locally before relying on them in Syria.

    Root causes — windshield seal, cowl drainage, and intake-window suction, in combination. Heavy rain can cause water to enter the cabin through the driver-side air intake window feeding the HVAC system — a distinct failure mode from the A/C-condensate and sunroof-drain issues documented on the condensate-drainage card. This is not caused by one single defect but by three compounding factors: (1) windshield seal deterioration, which lets water flow down the A-pillar toward the intake area; (2) the hood/cowl (жабо) having a poor drainage design that doesn't clear water fast enough; (3) the ventilation intake window being positioned low (roughly 1.5–2 cm above the surrounding surface) and creating a pressure differential at high fan speed that actively sucks water in rather than merely allowing it to pool. Because all three factors compound, a fix that addresses only one of them (e.g. resealing the windshield alone) may not fully resolve the leak.1234567

    Temporary/partial fixes tested by owners, with trade-offs. (a) A side shield blocking direct water entry at the intake window reduces the leak but also reduces airflow into the HVAC system — a real trade-off, not a free fix. (b) A top cover/visor over the intake increases aerodynamic disturbance (wind noise/drag) and was less effective than the side-shield approach. (c) An inexpensive aquarium-siphon-style attachment (~200 RUB, RU market, March 2024) was partially effective at redirecting water away from the intake, but not a complete solution on its own.1234567

    Recommended proper fix — seal the cowl and redirect water at the intake. Rather than relying on the partial fixes above alone, the recommended durable approach is: (1) seal the hood/cowl area with butyl tape to stop the underlying drainage-design weakness; (2) fabricate a proper water-diversion shield with an angled design specifically shaped to channel water away from the intake window rather than just blocking it; (3) address the suction effect by reducing it where practical (e.g. being mindful of running max fan speed in the heaviest rain, since that's when the pressure-differential suction is strongest). Owners documented the water-intake pathway with video, using a garden hose and full ventilation-fan speed to simulate heavy rain — this test confirmed that side shields performed better than top shields at blocking the actual water path.1234567
