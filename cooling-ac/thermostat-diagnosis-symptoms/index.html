    Thermostat failure — symptoms and diagnosis

    Tags: #thermostat · #cooling · #overheating · #temperature · #fuse · #water-pump · #gasket

    Normal reference numbers. Thermostat should be marked to open the small (cabin/block) loop at 82°C (some batches 82–83°C) and reach full opening around 97–99°C, when the auxiliary cooling fans also engage. If a thermostat marked 82°C is instead opening around 96°C (verified by cluster reading with cold lower radiator hose), it is out of spec or defective — check for air pockets first, since trapped air can also cause misleading readings.12

    Idle temperature creep. A common complaint: temperature gauge rises above normal while stationary (on-ramp/traffic), then returns to normal under acceleration or load. This is typically a thermostat malfunction or a fan-operation issue — first check whether the cooling fan cycles properly; if it does, the thermostat is the likely culprit.3

    Slow warm-up + falling highway temp = stuck-open thermostat. If the temperature gauge doesn't rise properly during warm-up, then drops again at highway speed, the thermostat is likely stuck open — the large (radiator) loop stays active even when the engine is cold, which both slows warm-up and prevents reaching normal operating temperature.4

    High temp + cold lower hose = stuck-closed thermostat. If engine temperature exceeds 95°C while the lower radiator hose stays cold, the thermostat is likely stuck closed/failing, restricting coolant flow to the radiator. Test: feel the lower hose after the engine is warm and under load/acceleration; if it stays cold or barely warm, replace the thermostat. Cooling fans activate at ~98–99°C regardless of thermostat state, so fan operation alone doesn't rule out a stuck thermostat.567

    Hose-temperature diagnostic (general method). Upper hose (small loop) is always hot once the engine is warm. Feel the upper vs. lower hose: if there's a clearly noticeable temperature difference by hand, the thermostat is failing to open the large loop. If both hoses feel the same temperature, test the water pump instead (run the heater on full heat and check outlet temperature). If the pump also checks out normal, suspect an air pocket — bleed by opening the expansion tank cap and running the engine until air is audibly purged.87

    Verified-good baseline after a fresh thermostat. One owner's post-replacement readings via OBD-II: 82–83°C opens the large circuit, fan engages at 99°C, and steady highway cruising settles at 91–92°C — cited as the normal-function baseline to compare against.9

    Systematic fan/thermostat verification procedure: (1) connect an OBD adapter or digital gauge; (2) idle the vehicle and watch for fan engagement (should be 98–99°C); (3) fan should cool the engine back down to ~92°C before disengaging; (4) cycle time should be roughly 10–15 seconds; (5) erratic fluctuation or a fan that won't engage points to thermostat failure; (6) compare highway vs. city-traffic behavior. Pre-facelift models use an analog needle gauge (coarser resolution); facelift models show a digital read-out for more precise diagnosis.10

    Effect on cabin heat. If the heater blows cold at idle but warm at higher RPM, the most likely cause is the same stuck-open thermostat (large loop staying active, starving the heater circuit). Verify: at operating temperature, if the upper radiator hose is hot but cabin air stays cold, replace the thermostat. Also check for air pockets after any recent coolant work, and confirm the radiator cap is sealing properly.11

    Conflicting read on "not opening despite rated 82°C" case. One owner reported a thermostat rated at 82°C but showing 93–96°C on the gauge with a cold lower hose, even after a 20 km highway drive — suspected defective part or excessive flow bias toward the small loop (heater core, throttle body, block, head). Others in the community argued the thermostat's opening is gradual, not instant, and that readings up to ~96°C before full fan engagement can be normal — so a defective-part diagnosis should not be assumed from temperature reading alone without also checking the lower hose.12

    Fans not audibly engaging + cold radiator — additional stuck-closed test. A separate report of the stuck-closed pattern: engine temperature reached 95–100°C but the cooling fans did not audibly engage and the radiator stayed cold.6 Extra field test beyond feeling the lower hose: accelerate hard while the car is stationary — if the lower radiator hose heats up immediately, the thermostat has very likely failed (stuck closed, restricting flow to the large loop).13 Community note on failure timing: thermostat failure after roughly 7 years of operation is described as a common/expected failure point on this car, not unusual.14

    Cabin-heat loss as the primary symptom, with failure-mileage examples. A thermostat can fail in a way that shows up mainly as a complete loss of cabin heating rather than an engine-temperature warning: signs are the engine not heating up normally, coolant hoses feeling warm while the radiator itself stays cool, and the temperature gauge reading low/stuck.15 Reported failure mileages: 42,000 km (Fill) and 89,540 km (Andrey).1615 Replacement in these cases required removing the intake manifold (or, per some advice, the power-steering pump instead — see the part-numbers card for the access-route debate); a Luzar thermostat was used for the replacement.1718 A simple field verification test for a suspect part before replacement: drop the thermostat in boiling water and watch whether its shaft/pin visibly extends as it heats — a working unit should visibly actuate.19

    Continuous fan running — full diagnostic path, including a fuse case. Symptom: cooling fan runs continuously even after the engine reaches normal operating temperature (87–90°C) and never shuts off. Diagnostic path: (1) verify A/C/climate isn't enabled (A/C on legitimately keeps a fan running), (2) check both radiator hose temperatures — the upper hose should be hot, the lower warm-or-cold depending on thermostat state, (3) possible causes in order: a thermostat stuck partially open, a blocked/dirty radiator, a failing water pump, or a faulty coolant-temperature sensor. In one confirmed case here, a blown 30A fuse (Fuse 1, located under the driver-side area) was the actual cause — not the thermostat at all, so check fuses before assuming a parts failure. In another case, thermostat replacement (Luzar brand) resolved it. A separate symptom pointer: if the ECU throws the sensor into limp mode because of a faulty coolant-temperature-sensor reading, it will force the fan on as a protective default.2021222324252627282930

    Water pump vs. thermostat — telling them apart. Water pump failure signs: bearing noise, visible coolant seepage at the pump body, and elevated coolant temperature. Thermostat failure (stuck partially open) is more common and looks different: sustained 99°C with the fans running continuously, then temperature drops when RPM increases. A thermostat sticking is a red flag specifically when fans run continuously after warm-up — that pattern points to the thermostat, not the pump. Caveat: even a freshly installed thermostat may take 1–2 weeks to fully "clear" and settle into normal behavior — don't immediately re-diagnose a brand-new part as faulty. Quick field check: if the coolant hose feels warm and the level stays stable, the pump is likely healthy — focus diagnosis on the thermostat first. Secondary checks if the thermostat also checks out: radiator-core plugging, or the cooling-fan fuse (a recent, separate case: 30A and 40A fuses under the hood, and replacing the 30A fuse resolved a constant-fan complaint — consistent with the fuse-first lesson above).3132333435363738394041

    Full overheating troubleshooting checklist (93–99°C highway, target 82–89°C). A systematic order for chasing overheating, useful as a top-level checklist: (1) confirm the thermostat's rated opening temperature (82°C) against actual behavior — a spike to 99°C on the highway against an ~89°C target is abnormal; (2) check the thermostat itself for defects — disassembly testing is the reliable way, since defective units are common; (3) verify water-pump operation — a failing pump causes an instant overheat spike within seconds of driving, a distinctive tell vs. the slower thermostat pattern; (4) inspect for radiator fouling — clean the fins, particularly relevant in dusty climates; (5) rule out an air lock in the cooling system — described as the primary cause of unexplained overheating in this thread; the expel method is to remove the throttle-body inlet hose with the engine running until coolant flows uninterrupted (typically 2–3 minutes); (6) as a last resort, consider a head-gasket leak if air keeps appearing in the system despite bleeding — confirm with an OBD2 gas analyzer or a UV coolant-dye trace test.42434445464748495051525354555657

    Thermostat opening temperature — replace above 97°C even if "working". Reiterating and sharpening the baseline spec above: factory thermostat opens at 82°C; normal highway operating temperature is 82–89°C. Temperatures sustained above 97°C warrant thermostat replacement even if the unit still appears to be functioning at all — don't wait for total failure before swapping a marginal part.585960

    Bench-test technique: gut the thermostat to eliminate it as a variable. To definitively determine whether overheating is caused by the thermostat, a more invasive diagnostic than the boiling-water test above: remove the internal spring and valve from the thermostat housing entirely, using a grinder to carefully cut away all the moving parts around the spring. This creates two permanently open circuits (small and large loop), removing the thermostat as a variable while keeping the housing itself functional and installed — useful specifically to isolate whether the thermostat (vs. pump, air pocket, or head gasket) is the actual cause before buying a replacement part. Caution: the thermostat is mounted on two welded studs that will break during this removal process, so this is a destructive/one-way modification for diagnostic purposes, not a reversible test.6162636465666768697071727374

    Fan engage/disengage thresholds, restated with disengage point. Thermostat starts opening at 82°C; full opening at 97°C. Cooling fan engages at 99°C and disengages at 95°C — adds a confirmed disengage threshold to the fan-cycling numbers noted elsewhere on this card.75

    Multiple thermostat swaps often needed before finding a reliable unit. Several owners independently report buying 4–5 different thermostats before finding one that works reliably — thermostat behavior (opening temperature, response time) is described as inconsistent between individual units, even nominally identical ones. A compounding factor: many mechanics and owners don't follow proper air-bleeding procedures after each swap, which makes it hard to tell a genuinely bad part from an unbled system — bleed thoroughly (see the coolant-capacity card) before concluding a newly installed thermostat is itself defective.767778

    CAT vs. Luzar — opposite failure modes, same symptom family. Two brand-specific failure reports with opposite behavior, useful for triage: a CAT thermostat failed stuck closed (no coolant flow to the large loop at all); a Luzar thermostat on a different car failed stuck open (permanently running the cool circuit, causing highway temperature to drop 40–50°C below normal and a weak/cold heater). These are opposite failure directions from the same part category, so "the thermostat failed" alone doesn't tell you which symptom to expect — check actual temperature/hose behavior to determine which failure mode you have. Recommendation given both brands failing in this thread: try a third thermostat or a different brand if the first replacement doesn't resolve the issue; also check the coolant-temperature sensor, which can read incorrectly and mimic a thermostat fault. Mann Filter thermostats were mentioned positively as an alternative worth trying.7980818283

    Large-loop bypass failure traced to a perforated cooling line, not just air. One owner's overheating case (large cooling loop not engaging, immediate overheat) took two weeks at a first service shop without resolution. A second shop found the actual root cause: a cooling line with a hole in it, which was both leaking and letting air into the circuit — replacing that line plus the thermostat resolved the issue. Lesson for hard-to-diagnose cases: if standard thermostat/pump/air-bleed checks don't resolve persistent overheating, physically inspect the cooling lines themselves for small perforations, not just the major components.8485

    Thermostat brand/rating differences — Azur early failure, Ford 86°C vs Mazda 94–96°C. An Azur brand thermostat failed after only 8,000 km; replaced with a Strong brand unit. Separately, different thermostat models are noted to have different rated opening temperatures: Ford (86°C) vs. Mazda (94–96°C) cross-compatible units. For X80 owners specifically chasing overheating, a Ford 86°C-rated thermostat is the community-preferred choice for a lower operating target — though some owners report even genuine Ford-spec units still opening at the higher Mazda-like temperature if the part isn't authentic, i.e. counterfeits are a real risk in this category. Always verify the cooling circuit's overall integrity (leaks, air pockets) whenever replacing a thermostat, regardless of brand chosen.8687888990919293949596

    Diagnosing thermostat failure with a cold heater — closed vs. open positions. Engine overheating combined with a cold cabin heater points to the thermostat stuck closed: coolant circulation to the radiator is blocked, but this does not necessarily affect the cabin heater circuit's own small loop, so the two symptoms (hot engine, cold heater) can co-occur. Diagnostic check: feel whether the radiator hose is warm while the heater blows cold — if the hose stays cold, that supports a stuck-closed thermostat. Alternative causes to rule out: an air pocket (завоздушенность) in the system, low coolant level, or a failed water pump. The thermostat can stick in either position, each with a distinct symptom profile: stuck closed → engine runs hot, heater runs cold; stuck open → slow engine warm-up, heater works, but the engine overheats from excessive/constant large-loop circulation. Live disagreement recorded here: some owners argue a thermostat stuck closed can prevent heater warmth entirely if a steam pocket forms in the small loop; others argue the heater circuit is plumbed independently of thermostat position and should always receive some warmth regardless. Community consensus: the thermostat remains the first, most likely suspect either way, but a service visit is recommended for a conclusive diagnosis in ambiguous cases.979899100101102103104105106107108109110111112

    Gasket-thickness mismatch after a thermostat swap causes a new air leak. If air leaks develop specifically after a thermostat replacement (not before), check the new gaskets' thickness against the originals — a mismatch is a distinct, separate failure mode from the more common causes above. The housing job typically requires 4 cylinder-head gaskets plus 1 round seal, and all of them must be the same height; if the replacement gaskets are thicker than the originals, they create air gaps at the joint that let air back into the system no matter how well you bleed it. Fix: source original-spec gaskets, or carefully verify that any aftermarket set matches the original height spec before installing.113114115116117

    Excessive city-driving fan operation — a second confirming case for thermostat-first diagnosis. Cooling fan running excessively during city driving, only stopping when the engine is switched off (including while parked/idling) is again traced to thermostat malfunction — replacing the thermostat resolved it, consistent with the fuse-vs-thermostat pattern discussed above (check the fuse first, but expect thermostat to be the more common root cause). Related tip while you're in there: inspect the crankcase-ventilation hose for cracks, since a cracked hose can introduce its own separate air leak that muddies the diagnosis after thermostat work.113114118

    Thermostat opening spec, restated with a stuck-open symptom example. X80 thermostat should open at 82°C; both OEM and aftermarket units at this spec are available. If the engine instead runs unusually cool (e.g. 60°C at cruising speed), suspect the thermostat is stuck open or otherwise faulty — a useful complement to the more commonly discussed stuck-closed/overheating pattern.119120121

    Stuck-closed at low RPM specifically — a distinctive low-speed pattern. A specific stuck-closed variant: the thermostat sticks closed only at low/idle RPM — the fan triggers at 99°C and cuts at 95°C as normal, but coolant doesn't actually circulate (lower radiator hose stays cold) at low RPM. At high RPM, pump pressure is enough to force some circulation through the stuck valve anyway, and temperature drops back to ~82°C. Diagnostic tell: if the lower hose is cold but the fan is running and OBD reads ~100°C, the thermostat is stuck closed and needs replacement — don't just address the fan noise/behavior, replace the actual failed part. A related but distinct possible contributor raised in this thread: a clogged heater-radiator (heater core) as a remaining cause after thermostat replacement, or the independent heater circuit being disable-able as a workaround in some configurations — noted as open/unresolved side threads rather than confirmed fixes.122123124125126127

    Thermostat replacement cost note — brand reliability complaint plus a reference thread. One owner reports replacing a second Luzar thermostat after the first failed, characterizing the brand as unreliable in their experience (contrast with the positive Luzar mentions on the parts card — document as another live brand-quality disagreement). The original FAW thermostat is generally preferred when available but costs more than Chinese aftermarket alternatives. A detailed community comparison thread on thermostat options exists on Drive2.ru: https://www.drive2.ru/l/636541917009562998/128129130

    Cross-model reference — Grand Vitara Club thread on a near-identical symptom. Flagged as relevant because the X80 shares Suzuki Grand Vitara suspension architecture: a Grand Vitara Club forum thread describes a thermostat only opening above 4,000 RPM during spirited driving, staying stuck closed during normal driving, with the fan running but the lower hose staying cold, and discusses heater-radiator clogging as a related cause — the same diagnostic shape documented across several X80 cases above. Thread: https://grand-vitara-club.ru/threads/sgv-2-4-2011g-termostat-otkryvaetsja-tolko-pri-oborotax-4000-pri-progonke-pri-dvizhenii-zakryt-postojanno-ventiljatory-rabotajut-pomenjal-uzhe-vse-kryshku.19181/131132

    Failure-mileage spread and radiator fouling as the more common root cause. Further failure-mileage data points, corroborating the mileage examples already on this card: an OEM thermostat failed at 47,000 km; a Ford aftermarket unit at 67,000 km; another failure was reported as late as 280,000 km (traced to a "proclasket" — internal gasket/seal — failure rather than the valve mechanism itself). Community pattern across these cases: radiator fouling, not a defective thermostat, is frequently the actual root cause — many owners mistake air pockets or blocked/dirty cooling passages for a thermostat problem. Practical implication: before condemning the thermostat on symptoms alone, clean the radiator and check antifreeze quality — genuine online-counterfeit thermostats exist, but radiator cleaning and quality antifreeze more often resolve overheating complaints than a thermostat swap.133134135

    Temperature-gauge instability traced to a corroded sensor connector, not the thermostat. If the temperature gauge shows unstable readings or reads unusually cold, check the thermostat-housing electrical connector before replacing the thermostat itself: oxidation/green corrosion on the coolant-temperature-sensor terminal connector causes poor electrical contact, which produces erratic gauge behavior that mimics a cooling problem. Fix: inspect the connector for green corrosion and clean the terminals — try this before swapping the thermostat.136137

    Water-passage housing crack at the temperature-sensor mount — a related but distinct failure from the thermostat itself. A separate, common failure point near the thermostat: the aluminum/polyamide water-cooling housing where the coolant-temperature sensor mounts (part 1303010-37K) develops cracks, causing a coolant leak. The crack forms in the polyamide-plastic/aluminum compound housing material and cannot be repaired with sealant — the housing must be replaced outright. Some owners report this failure as early as ~45,000 km; root cause is the polyamide material decomposing under sustained engine heat over time, i.e. a temperature-related durability issue rather than a manufacturing defect in any one part.138139140141142

    Thermostat-housing flange leak under the intake manifold — reported as very common (70–80%). A separate community report (from the other X80 Telegram group) puts a number on the housing-leak pattern already discussed on this card: an estimated 70–80% of X80s develop coolant seepage from the thermostat-housing flange, located under the intake manifold, after 50,000+ km. The housing bolts on below the intake manifold with a single gasket; the gasket alone cannot be sourced separately — the entire thermostat assembly must be replaced. Leaks are reported to recur even after a quality replacement, and the job requires intake-manifold removal (high labor), consistent with the access-route debate on the parts-numbers card. Suggested replacement options: OEM Mazda 6 thermostat, OEM Ford Focus thermostat, or the Motorad-brand Chinese equivalent.143144145146147

    Overheating with visible coolant evaporation near the intake manifold — suspected T-junction connector. A related but separately reported overheating case: engine overheating combined with visible coolant evaporation/leaking near the intake manifold, in the exhaust-side area. Community suspicion centers on the T-junction cooling connector that supplies coolant to the heater, engine block, and radiator circuits — a leak at this connector could produce exactly this symptom pattern. Live disagreement/uncertainty: the exact failure point was not conclusively pinned down in this report, and the consensus was that disassembly is likely required to confirm whether it's the T-junction, the thermostat housing (see the flange-leak note above), or another nearby component.148149150151152153
