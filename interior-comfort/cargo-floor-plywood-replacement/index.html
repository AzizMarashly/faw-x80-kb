    Trunk cargo floor — plywood replacement

    Tags: #trunk · #diy

    The factory plastic cargo floor degrades and cracks over time. DIY replacement: use 12 mm plywood (chosen over 10 mm since the price was about the same at time of purchase) with a self-adhesive carpet layer (graphite color) on top. The plywood sheet fits within the original floor's 6 mm depth recess. If the plywood has cross-ribs underneath for rigidity, even an 8 mm sheet performs far better than the original degraded plastic. Fastening: a stapler works for attaching the carpet layer — use a stapler with sharp staples, since dull staples bend instead of driving in cleanly 12.

    Reinforcement/repair options for the stock plastic floor (without replacing it): the stock plastic cargo floor flexes and develops cracks (2–4 common failure points) under cargo load. Three alternative approaches reported: (1) spare-wheel platform — place the spare wheel on a 1–1.5 cm-thick platform underneath, secured using the wheel bolts; the wheel-storage area then doubles as a rigid support that prevents flex; (2) foam/insulation reinforcement — insert foam/polystyrene insulation strips into the gaps where the floor edges meet the seat frames; (3) crack repair — repair existing cracks with fiberglass putty before applying either reinforcement method above 3456789.

    OSB-3 floor with a hinged tire-access panel: an alternative DIY cargo floor uses 9mm OSB-3 board instead of plywood — 625×1250mm sheets (sold e.g. by LemanPro) fit well with the large rear seat folded down; 11mm OSB is also usable. The design adds a hinged external access panel (using a piano hinge) over the spare-wheel area so the wheel remains reachable without removing the whole floor; a support bar is attached at the rear-seat fold point. Reinforcement notes: backing/reinforcing bars are attached with the same fasteners as the hinge to prevent the hinge from tearing out under load, and compensating spacers are added under the hinge area to distribute load evenly. Dimensions should be measured in-place to match the trunk's contours rather than assumed from a template. The resulting floor supports weight but has some flex; proper bracing (as above) prevents hinge failure during loading — one owner suggested repositioning the hinges differently and offered to take precise measurements to share with the group. A telescoping/sliding design was mentioned as a possible future improvement 1011121314151617181920.
